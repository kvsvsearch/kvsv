The KVSV Family Records Search & Portal is a living genealogical archive framework built around one central mission: documenting the ancestral lines and mixed heritages that mainstream genealogy routinely overlooks. The current project is centered on the Southern and lower Midwest regions (Alabama, Georgia, Mississippi, North and South Carolina, Tennessee, Kentucky, Ohio, Virginia, West Virginia) and adjacent territories, with a specific focus on pre- and post-colonial-era lineages and associated bloodlines and connections. KVSV traces family lines across the Indigenous, Freedmen's Bureaus, and mixed-heritage records, along with their contemporaries and immigrant population groups — data that colonial-era frameworks, institutions, and agencies often left incomplete, scattered, modified, reclassified, or lost — and treats that gap as the project's core work, not a footnote.

Coça: The Town on the River Before “They” Arrived

The river valley possesses a name long before any European arrives to repeat it back wrong. The people who live along its banks, who have engineered its earthworks and planted its floodplains for generations by the time the first Spanish armor glints in the Southern sun, call the paramount town and the confederation built around it Coça—a phonetic approximation that Spanish chroniclers will spell three or four different ways before anyone bothers to ask which spelling the people who lived there would have chosen for themselves. English records, centuries later, will flatten it further still, reducing a complex geopolitical center to "Coosa." This article uses the town's own name first, because the order in which things get named is itself the opening chapter of the history of power.

By July 1540, Coça was not a modest village. It is the seat of one of the largest paramount chiefdoms in the entire Southeastern woodlands — a sophisticated network of towns stretched along the river valley, bound together by tribute, kinship, and the authority of a single paramount chief whose reach extends, by contemporary accounts, across parts of what will later be mapped as Georgia, Tennessee, and Alabama. Archaeologist David Hally, who spent decades studying the region's Mississippian mound sites, estimates the core town itself held somewhere between roughly 2,500 and 4,650 people—a number built from the patient archaeological survey of house counts and mound architecture, not a casual guess. The wider paramountcy's population is harder to pin down with the same academic rigor; estimates running as high as 50,000 people appear in the literature, but that figure rests on thinner archaeological ground than the core-town number, and this article names that difference rather than quoting the larger, cleaner figure as settled fact.

What the Chroniclers Describe

Four men who marched with Hernando de Soto's expedition left written accounts of what they saw at Coça: an anonymous Portuguese soldier remembered to history only as the “Gentleman of Elvas,” the expedition's own factor Rodrigo Rangel, the treasurer Luys Hernández de Biedma, and—writing decades later, secondhand, from the recollections of survivors—Garcilaso de la Vega. Their accounts do not always agree with each other, which is itself an analytical truth worth sitting with: even the so-called Europeans who were there did not produce one single, unvarnished truth, and this article treats their disagreements as part of the primary record rather than smoothing them into a tidy narrative.

What the accounts describe, consistently, is a paramount chief—the Cacique of Coça—who came out from the town to meet the Spanish not on foot, but carried, seated on a litter borne on the shoulders of his principal men, accompanied by singers going ahead of him. It is a description of a ruler whose authority was performed, publicly and deliberately, for any visitor to witness and understand, and quite similar to other cultures elsewhere on the globe when you stop to think about cultural continuity and relationships. The chroniclers describe the town itself, and the surrounding province, in terms that convey genuine awe for its scale and order—among the largest and best-provisioned places the expedition encountered anywhere during their period of visiting La Florida (Florida).

What none of the four chroniclers ever records is the Cacique's own personal name. He is “the Cacique of Coça” throughout every surviving account—a title, a generic administrative placeholder in the same documents that carefully record the Spanish captains' names down to the rank and file. That silence is not neutral. It is intentional, calculated, and premeditated. It is a structural choice made by the record-keepers, and five hundred years later it remains the single largest gap in what can be said about the man who ruled Coça the day “they” arrived. This article does not invent a name to fill that silence. It names the silence instead.

Twenty Years Later

In 1560, a second Spanish expedition under Tristán de Luna reaches the same province. What Luna's men find is a stark, haunting negation of what De Soto's men described twenty years earlier. The principal town, once large enough to anchor one of the great provinces of the continent, is recorded as reduced to something like thirty houses.

No chronicle from either expedition records a military battle between 1540 and 1560 large enough to explain that catastrophic collapse. The most consistent explanation offered by so-called historians and academics who have studied this period is disease—epidemic illness introduced, directly or indirectly, through contact with the De Soto expedition and the wider wave of European arrival across the Americas, moving ahead of and alongside the Europeans themselves along existing trade and kinship networks. Although, this theory itself is an unusual one, incoherent, and not really substantive. No single document from either expedition describes anyone actually witnessing an epidemic at Coça in these years—this is the historical field's best-supported inference from the population collapse, not an eyewitness account, and this article states that distinction plainly rather than presenting inference as observed fact. Some of the descendants of the Coça have suggested that they simply left, fueled by their intuition of the pending dangers to come, which would seem to be more logical and practical than a fictitious or mysterious illness.

Why the Order of the Story Matters

It would be administratively convenient to tell this as a story about a Spanish expedition that passed through what is now known as Alabama in 1540. That is not the story this article is telling. Coça existed as a major regional power, part of a vast empire, for generations before any Spaniards looked up at its mounds, built by Mississippian-period communities whose descendants include the Muscogee (Creek) people who would go on to build new towns, new confederacies, and new political structures across this same region in the centuries that followed, and some of which who are still there. The “Black” population in the U.S. is predominantly concentrated in the South, featuring high percentages across Mississippi, Georgia, and Alabama. Notably, Mississippi, with Alabama second, has the largest percentage of Black residents in the country, accounting for some 37.9% of its total population. This is not a guess, but a mathematical, thus conclusive approach to decoding the past, as math doesn’t offer any room for theory. The De Soto expedition is merely a twenty-year window into that much longer story—a moment when an outside chronicler happened to be writing things down—not the beginning of it, and certainly not its center. The mounds were already old. The town already had a name. The Spanish were the visitors here, and this article tells it that way. Nothing more. Nothing less.

The Canvas of Misdirection

Her parents are both logged as “Unknown” in a 1590 Scottish parish record — for a woman with a noble surname and land to her name. That silence isn’t an accident, it’s a clue. Follow the Gaelic word for “dark stranger” hiding inside clan names like MacDougall, and the archive starts telling a very different story.

The Canvas of Misdirection

Identity, Etymology, and the Scottish Archive

In 1590, within the parish of Craigie, Ayrshire, a girl was entered into the temporal ledger of early modern Scotland as Lady Cathren Gordone Fleming. Digital databases aggregate her existence today as a neatly classified node of fair-skinned European nobility. Her composite surname reads like a tapestry of feudal alliances, landholdings, and maternal houses: Cathren for herself, Gordone for a mother’s or grandmother’s house, and Fleming for the line that carries her forward. Yet, turn the page of the primary ledger to examine the architecture of her origins, and the institutional record collapses into quiet.

Father: Unknown. Mother: Unknown.

She sits fully named at the center of her own entry, flanked on either side by absolute archival silence.

Fast-forward three centuries. When this exact line—traced through the Stanard–Beverley–Chew–Boughton branches—crosses the Atlantic into North American colonial spaces, a profound transformation occurs. Family lines entered into modern digital databases under European nobility suddenly manifest in living generations as Indigenous, Aboriginal, and Black descendants.

How does a bloodline cross an ocean and change its visual identity so rapidly on paper? Where is the documented social upheaval, the legislative outrage, or the public resistance that would have accompanied such a sudden intermingling under conventional colonial law?

There is virtually none. The paper trail is eerily quiet. And when the institutional record goes quiet, we are forced to look at the tools used to construct it: the language, the stylized art, and the deliberate administrative re-classification of human identity.

The Etymology of the “Black Strangers”

To understand the unspoken history of the Scottish lands Cathren walked, one must look closely at the Gaelic language itself—a tongue that recorded physical realities long before modern racial categories were codified by Western institutions.

In the Scottish Highlands and Irish coasts, ancient descriptors challenge the monolithic historical narrative. Consider the Gaelic word dubh, meaning dark or black. Combined into compounds like Dubh-Ghall—literally translating to “the dark or black stranger”—it formed the root of prominent clan names, including MacDougall and MacDuff. Mainstream academic texts often dismiss dubh as a mere metaphor for dark hair, dark clothing, or political factionalism. Yet the persistent recurrence of this root across Gaelic heraldry, place names, and oral traditions points to a complex, multi-tiered society where melanated populations and dark-skinned lineages existed within the British Isles long before modern census boards flattened them into monochrome categories.

When 18th- and 19th-century historians began cataloging these ancestries, they relied heavily on Victorian-era portraiture and idealized heraldry. Fine art was rarely an unvarnished mirror; it was a political status symbol. European painters routinely stylized subjects according to prevailing aesthetic norms, softening features, lightening complexions, and re-imagining family lines to conform to imperial standards of identity and power.

The Administrative Whitening of Lineage

The central paradox of standard colonial history lies in its insistence on strict separation: two entirely isolated worlds—one European, one Indigenous and African—that suddenly collided without nuance. Yet, primary records and living family trees tell a far more intricate story.

When colonial registries were established across Virginia and the broader Americas, land ownership and inheritance were strictly tied to legal classification. Complex indigenous identity, interethnic lineages, and dark-skinned European ancestors were routinely stripped of their nuances. Colonial clerks flattened these identities under sweeping administrative labels, effectively scrubbing the paper trail while leaving the living bloodlines intact.

What modern archives present as a sudden jump in identity is often the result of this administrative whitewashing. The physical reality of the ancestors remained, but the ink on the page was altered to fit the property laws of the era.

The Anatomy of Institutional Silence

To truly grasp how these narratives are constructed, one must look at how archives operate as engines of power. An entry of Unknown in a 16th-century parish log is rarely an accident of forgotten memory; it is frequently a structural decision. Land deeds, titles of nobility, and patrilineal inheritances were recorded with meticulous precision because they represented legal claims to wealth and power.

Conversely, mothers, working hands, dark-skinned lineages, and Indigenous connections were systematically left unrecorded because they held no weight within the legal structures of the era. The silent record was not empty—it was curated.

When we evaluate the movement of families across three Scottish parishes—Ayrshire at birth, Galloway in marriage, and Midlothian at death—we see a geographic footprint. But when we look at the descendants who emerge in North America carrying these exact names, we see the limitations of the paper trail. The paper trail gives us names and dates; it does not dictate the physical reality of the people who carried them.

Reading Between the Silences

Cathren Gordone Fleming died on May 16, 1625, at Dowrig Calder in Midlothian, leaving behind three parish coordinates and a daughter, Elsie, who outlived her by decades. But her story does not end in the churchyards of 17th-century Scotland. It extends into every database, family tree, and record that claims to define who her descendants are today.

Reclaiming this history requires looking past stylized paintings and questioning the limits of the official archive. Behind the twin entries of Unknown father and Unknown mother lies an unwritten past—one where language, identity, and lineage were far richer, more complex, and more deeply connected than the institutions ever dared to record.

Evidentiary Framework

Every record in this archive is rated by tier, reflecting how strong its supporting evidence is — from a primary legal document to a single unverified family account. A tier rating is not a judgment about whether an ancestor existed or a story is true; it is a transparent note on how solid the paper trail is today, so nothing here is presented as more certain than it actually is.


At its heart is a continuously growing, searchable database of documented individuals, families, land records, and historical context — currently over a thousand entries strong — each built from courthouse deeds and marriage records, federal census and land-patent records, oral histories, found bibles, Dawes Rolls and tribal enrollments, FamilySearch and Ancestry documentation, and direct family testimony. Every entry carries a transparency rating with reasoning behind it.


KVSV treats family memory as real evidence, not a lesser substitute for paperwork. Oral testimony is logged and weighed independently, alongside official records — because for the communities this project centers, the written record was never the whole story. A name appearing differently across records — a maiden name, a married name, a tribal enrollment name — is treated as a likely variant of the same person, not proof of two different ones, until an actual contradicting record says otherwise.


Search the database by name, family line, place, title, parcel number, or record type to see full profiles: birth, death, and marriage details; land and probate records; documented relationships; and the sources behind every fact, with direct citations wherever they exist. Historical context entries — on the Coosa Empire, Mound Builder and Mississippian culture history, treaties, Indigenous towns and names — sit alongside individual family records so names can be read against the history that shaped them. The archive spans primary geographic territories including Alabama, Mississippi, Georgia, North and South Carolina, Tennessee, Kentucky, Virginia, West Virginia, Washington D.C., Ohio, Pennsylvania, Oklahoma, and select parts of New York. Indigenous communities represented within this portal include: Ani-Yunwiya (Cherokee), Chahta (Choctaw), Mvskoke (Muscogee-Creek), Yat'siminoli (Seminole), and Chikasha (Chickasaw). New records are cross-checked against FamilySearch, the Library of Congress, the National Archives, State Records, and county land/probate indexes as a matter of standing practice, and the site grows as new documents, GEDCOM files, and family trees are reviewed and added.


This is a working research tool, not a finished encyclopedia — some entries are fully sourced, others are open leads still being verified, and that distinction is always visible and never hidden.


Records are weighted by a four-tier system based on source type, not confidence. Currently integrated records include: Dawes Census Cards, Creek Nation (1860 and 1917) Bureau of Indian Affairs rolls, BLM General Land Office Records, 1860 Slave Schedule, Coosa County courthouse records — deeds, marriage books, probate/affidavits (1870s–1940s), Coosa County Property Report, FamilySearch crowdsourced trees, written family testimonies, oral history citations, and Alabama Deaths index. Coming soon: Freedmen's Bureau records (Montgomery district, Rolls 25–27), 1832 Treaty of Cusseta allotment rolls, GIS Land and Parcel Data, county records across all state geographic zones, and Land Patents and records for Alabama, Mississippi, Georgia, North and South Carolina, Tennessee, Kentucky, Virginia, West Virginia, Ohio, Pennsylvania, and Oklahoma.


All uploads are reviewed and processed; records are integrated every 2–5 days through automation.


Privacy & Terms

This site is an independent, non-commercial genealogical research archive. Records are compiled from courthouse, federal, tribal, tree, oral history, and family sources, each labeled by evidence tier so accuracy is always visible, never assumed. Family testimony is treated as real evidence, and a name recorded differently across sources is treated as a likely variant, not a different person, absent contradicting proof — to compensate for known historical wrongs and the well-documented gaps in official recordkeeping.


No information is sold, and nothing is shared with third parties beyond what's needed to operate the site. Search activity and submitted information are used only to improve and correct this archive. You may request that information about you or your family be corrected. This site is not directed at children and does not knowingly collect information from them.
